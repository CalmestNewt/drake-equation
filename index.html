Green Bank, West Virginia · November 1961

How many civilizations are listening right now?

Frank Drake wrote his equation the week before the first SETI meeting — not to predict an answer, but to set the agenda. Each term was a session on the schedule. Turn the seven dials and watch the estimate move across sixteen orders of magnitude.

=

Starting points

Four sets of assumptions, all argued in print by serious people, disagreeing by a factor of ten billion.

The cascade

The running product after each term, on a logarithmic axis. This is where the equation earns its keep: you can see which single assumption is doing all the work.

What that would mean

Three of the seven terms now rest on measurements. Infrared surveys fixed the star formation rate, microlensing and Kepler settled the planet count, and η⊕ has an error bar you can at least write down. The remaining four are the same open questions they were in 1961 — and the last of them, L, has exactly one data point. We are it, and we are about a century in.

Which is the equation's real result. Drag L alone across its range and N moves by ten orders of magnitude while everything else stays put. The question "is anyone out there?" turns out to be mostly the question "does anyone last?"

Sources: Licquia & Newman 2015 (ApJ 806:96) · Cassan et al. 2012 (Nature 481:167) · Bryson et al. 2021 (AJ 161:36). Nearest-neighbour figures assume civilizations spread evenly through a galactic disk of radius 50,000 ly and thickness 1,000 ly.
